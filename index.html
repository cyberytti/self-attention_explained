Self-attention

Introduction:

Every time you ask ChatGPT a question, watch your keyboard autocomplete a sentence, or read a machine-translated page, a Transformer model is working behind the scenes — and at the heart of every Transformer lies one key idea: self-attention. It is the mechanism that lets a model look at a word and decide which other words in the sentence it should pay attention to in order to understand that word correctly. This idea is so central to modern AI that the famous 2017 paper built entirely around it was fittingly titled “Attention Is All You Need”.

In this article, we will learn the concept of self-attention: what it is, what problem it solves, how it works, and an in-depth explanation of the mathematical operations behind it. We will start from the basics — why computers need numbers instead of words and how words are turned into vectors — and then build up, step by step, to a complete numerical example of a self-attention layer that you can follow and verify yourself. By the end, you will know exactly what happens inside self-attention and why it became the foundation of today’s language models.

The problem self-attention solves

We will start from the basics and first understand the problem for which self-attention is created. Now, we all know the fact that computers can’t understand raw text; not only text, they can’t understand images, videos, audio, etc. We know this, and we also know that computers can only understand numbers. This is where everything starts. Many of us know that the main applications of self-attention have been in the Transformer — specifically, the encoder-decoder Transformer for seq2seq tasks, mainly for language. Here, the first question arises

How to represent text into numbers?

In the early days, scientists used techniques like one-hot encoding, bag of words, etc. But the problem with these algorithms is that they are not that capable of representing the meaning of a particular word through numbers.

Then later they found techniques like text embeddings, where there is a neural network trained on a huge dataset to understand the meaning of particular words and their semantic relationships. Now it can easily tell that there is a difference between an apple and an orange, but somewhere they are related as well, because they are both fruits. These embedding models produce something called as “vectors”. Instead of representing a single word as a single integer, they represent a single word as a word vector, which can carry a lot more information about that word. After getting the embeddings for apple and orange, we can simply use some algorithm like cosine similarity to find how similar these two words are. Now, if I get the embedding of a car and compare the cosine similarity of these three words, and plot them on a graph, that graph would look something like this

Here, you can see the apple and orange have some distance, but they are still very close — it means that they have a very strong connection. But the car is far away from them; this means the car is not related to them.

After understanding what text/word embeddings do, you can think that the text embedding technique solves our first question. But there is a huge catch with it, and now things will get deeper.

See, it’s true that word embeddings can distinguish between an apple and a car, but all of these are static. It is not necessary that a word X will always mean the same thing — the meaning of the same word might change.

Let’s take an example:

I will write two sentences:

  1. An apple a day keeps the doctor away.
  1. The camera quality of apple is really good.

These two sentences contain the same word “apple”, but in the different sentences it holds a completely different meaning due to the rest of the context of the sentence. Here, word embeddings fail. Throughout the training, a word embedding model associates a single meaning with a single word; it can’t be changed depending on the context. For a word embedding model, the two “apple”s hold the same meaning. This is the problem self-attention solves.

How does self-attention solve this problem?

Self-attention was created for this purpose: to compute the actual meaning of a word depending on its context. The term “self-attention” means how much attention one word should give to other words — which word depends on whom, and how strong that dependency is. Self-attention produces a thing called “contextual embedding”. As the name suggests, this embedding also holds the information of the context, which simple word embeddings can’t do. The self-attention layer takes the word embeddings as input, adds contextual information to them, and returns the contextual embeddings.

Now, a normal question arises: how does self-attention do this? In the next section, we will get to know this.

Why are these weight matrices required?

The entire self-attention mechanism relies on Q, K, and V. To understand how strongly one word is related to another, we need these three components.

For example, when processing the word "Although", its Query (Q) is compared with the Keys (K) of all the other words to calculate how much attention it should pay to each word.

But the main question is: Who defines the roles of Q, K, and V inside the model? How does the model know that Q is supposed to make a query? And what does a query actually mean?

The answer is that initially, the model doesn't know any of these things! During training, the weight matrices get updated through backpropagation and gradually learn how to generate useful Queries, Keys, and Values.

Basically, we just define the mathematical formulas, such as:

Q = X × WQ​

Here, WQ​ is a learnable weight matrix. Through training, the model learns the values of these weights, which allows Q, K, and V to develop their respective roles in the self-attention mechanism.

After creating Q, K, and V, our next step is to calculate how much one word is related to the other words. Based on this relevance, we will decide how much attention one word should pay to the other words.

Remember, we discussed the roles of Q and K before. Q (Query) represents what a word is looking for, and K (Key) represents what a word has to offer.

These are the two main components we need to calculate the relevance between words.

Here, relevance means how closely a word's Query matches the Keys of all the other words. The higher the similarity score, the more relevant those words are to each other.

What is Scaling?

Scaling is a technique used to control the magnitude of values in embeddings or other numerical representations to keep training stable and improve the learning process.

In machine learning, scaling helps prevent numerical values from becoming excessively large or small, which can make optimization difficult.

Without proper scaling, we may encounter problems such as unstable gradients, exploding gradients, or vanishing gradients, depending on the model architecture and the operations being performed.

How Do We Perform Scaling?

To perform scaling in the attention mechanism, we divide the dot product of the Query (Q) and Key (K) matrices by the square root of the dimension of the Key vectors (dk​​).

After scaling, we do normalization. Before that, we have to know what normalization is and why we use it.

What is Normalization?

Normalization is a process of scaling values in a vector, matrix, or list so that their sum equals 1. This is commonly known as normalization using the L1 norm.

Why Do We Use Normalization?

Normalization helps maintain numerical stability and can make machine learning models train more effectively. Depending on the normalization technique, it can also help improve training stability.

This process is totally parallel — nothing here is sequential. Thus, by default, self-attention can’t catch the sequential (positional) information of a word in the sentence. That’s why we apply positional encoding before self-attention.

Conclusion:

Throughout this article, we learned what self-attention is, what it does, and how it works. We started with the fact that computers can only understand numbers, saw how word embeddings map words into vectors, and discovered their biggest weakness: they are static, so a word like “apple” gets the same vector no matter what the sentence is about. We then saw how self-attention solves this by producing contextual embeddings — each word’s Query is compared with every other word’s Key to get relevance scores, the scores are scaled and passed through softmax to become attention weights, and those weights are used to blend the Value vectors into the final output Y.

So the next time you see a model understand that “apple” means a fruit in one sentence and a tech company in another, remember that self-attention is doing that work — every word is simultaneously looking at every other word and deciding how much each one matters. This article explained single-head self-attention, but there are many variations of it, such as multi-head attention, which powers the full Transformer architecture.
